# Chapter 6 — The Computational and Network Revolution
## When information became executable and distance began to collapse

The industrial machine transformed energy into motion. The computer transformed information into operations.

That difference changed the trajectory of civilization.

Early electronic computers were enormous, expensive and specialized by modern standards, but they demonstrated a revolutionary principle: symbolic procedures could be executed automatically at electronic speed. Calculation no longer had to proceed at the pace of a human clerk or mechanical calculator.

The National Bureau of Standards, now NIST, was already working in the late 1940s on electronic computing as government agencies anticipated the value of machines capable of handling large quantities of data and lengthy calculations. The transistor and later integrated circuits helped make electronic computation smaller, more reliable and dramatically more scalable.

But hardware alone does not explain the revolution.

The general-purpose computer is unusual because its behavior can be changed through software. A physical machine can become a word processor, accounting system, scientific instrument, design environment, communication terminal or game without being rebuilt from scratch. Human intention can be expressed as instructions and executed repeatedly.

Software therefore became a new kind of machinery: machinery made primarily of logic.

Mainframes brought computation into governments, universities and large companies. Personal computers later moved it onto individual desks and into homes. Spreadsheets transformed business calculation. Databases changed record keeping. Word processors changed writing and editing. Computer-aided design altered engineering. Digital media changed how sound and images could be produced, copied and stored.

Then computers began talking to one another.

Networking transformed computation from isolated processing into shared infrastructure. ARPANET research in packet switching helped demonstrate that different computers could communicate across a network. By the end of 1969, four host computers formed the initial ARPANET. Work on internetworking eventually produced architectures in which independent networks could interconnect through common protocols.

This was conceptually profound. The network did not require every machine to be identical. Difference could survive beneath shared rules of communication.

Email quickly showed that people valued networks not only for sharing computers but for sharing ideas. The Internet Society's history notes that electronic mail became a major early network application and changed collaboration. The network was becoming a social space as well as a technical one.

The World Wide Web made this connected environment far easier to publish and navigate. CERN records that Tim Berners-Lee invented the Web there in 1989 to improve information sharing among scientists. On 30 April 1993, CERN placed the Web software in the public domain and later released it under an open licence, helping the technology spread broadly.

The Web changed the economics of publication. A person or organization connected to the network could make information available globally without owning a printing press or broadcast station. Search engines later made vast quantities of that information discoverable. E-commerce allowed transactions to occur across networks. Social platforms made ordinary users continuous producers as well as consumers of media.

The smartphone compressed many previous technologies into a portable networked computer: telephone, camera, map, library, music player, payment interface, sensor platform and gateway to software. Cloud computing moved large portions of storage and computation back into remote infrastructure while making them available on demand.

Humanity had constructed something unprecedented: a planetary information environment operating continuously.

This produced extraordinary benefits. Knowledge could cross borders almost instantly. Scientific collaboration accelerated. Small organizations gained access to infrastructure once reserved for large institutions. Families could communicate across continents. Education, entertainment and commerce became available through the same network.

But abundance created a new problem.

When information was scarce, the challenge was access. When information became effectively limitless, the challenge became attention and selection.

Algorithms increasingly decided what appeared first: search results, recommendations, advertisements, news feeds, products, videos and potential social connections. The network therefore evolved from a system that merely carried information into a system that ranked information.

That was the beginning of the algorithmic environment.

Before generative AI became widely visible, society had already built most of its prerequisites: global connectivity, enormous digital archives, cloud infrastructure, specialized chips, software distribution, online identity, digital payments and billions of users generating data.

The AI revolution did not descend onto an unprepared civilization. The computational and network revolutions built its nervous system.

The next threshold would be more unsettling. Computers had stored our information, executed our instructions and connected our minds. Increasingly, they would begin learning patterns from the information itself.
