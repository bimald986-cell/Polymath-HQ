# Chapter 6 — Generations Before the AI-Native World

Generational labels are useful only if we resist turning them into stereotypes.

Pew Research Center emphasizes that generational boundaries are analytical conventions rather than scientific laws. People born in the same period differ enormously by country, class, culture, race, gender, education, family and individual experience. The familiar labels are also especially shaped by U.S. demographic and cultural history. This chapter therefore uses them as one lens on technological change, not as universal descriptions of humanity.

## The Greatest and Silent generations

People born before and during the middle decades of the twentieth century encountered a world shaped by depression, global war, expanding industrial production, radio, mass newspapers and the rise of television. Pew commonly places the Silent Generation at 1928–1945 and earlier cohorts before it. Their formative environment was overwhelmingly analog. Information was comparatively scarce, broadcasting was centralized, and computing remained remote from ordinary household life.

## Baby Boomers

The Baby Boom generation is unusually tied to a measurable demographic event: the post-World War II surge in births. Pew uses 1946–1964. Boomers saw television become a mass medium, experienced enormous social and cultural change, and entered adulthood as electronics and computing began moving outward from governments, universities and large corporations.

## Generation X

Pew commonly defines Generation X as 1965–1980. This cohort crossed the analog-to-digital boundary. Many encountered childhood without the Web and adulthood with it. Personal computers, cable television, video games and early digital networks expanded during their formative decades. They became adults as computing and the internet moved from specialized systems toward ordinary work and home life.

## Millennials

Pew's widely used working range for Millennials is 1981–1996. This generation came of age during the expansion of the internet. For many, the transition from offline to online life happened while they were young enough to adapt rapidly but old enough to remember the earlier state. Search, email, instant messaging, social platforms, mobile phones and later smartphones changed how people found information, maintained relationships and entered work.

## Generation Z

Pew places the beginning of Generation Z after 1996 for analytical purposes while cautioning that such boundaries are not exact. Gen Z's formative world made digital connectivity increasingly ordinary. Smartphones, social media, streaming, algorithmic feeds and persistent online identity were not merely workplace technologies to adopt later in life. They became part of the environment of adolescence itself.

## Why this matters for the machine age

Across these cohorts we can see a compression in the interval between major information technologies. A person could be born into a radio-centered world and live to use smartphones and generative AI. Another could move from childhood without the Web to managing cloud-based organizations. A younger person might never remember a world without algorithmically selected information.

But the next transition is different in an important way. Earlier digital tools mostly expanded access, communication, storage and calculation. Generative and agentic systems increasingly participate in producing language, images, software, analysis and actions themselves.

That is the threshold this book now approaches.

Tomorrow's chapters should therefore stop treating history as merely a sequence of devices. The central variable becomes the changing division of cognitive and physical agency between people and machines.
